METHOD & LIMITATIONS

The calculation,
with its choices visible.

The current programs preserve the latest shared workflow while making smoothing, forecast timing, rounding and historical compatibility explicit. The source paper and the supplied workbooks describe related steps, but they are not identical specifications.

1. Annual input

Each region has an independent series of annual event dates. The CSV contains Year,DOY, with January 1 equal to day 1. Years must be consecutive, unique and ascending. Start years come from the records, not hardcoded plotting dates. The forecast year is the last observed year plus one.

2. FFT and the selected period

The FFT is applied to the observed DOYs. The zero-frequency bin is excluded, and the largest-power positive-frequency bin supplies the dominant period through the inverse of its frequency. The period is rounded half up to a whole-year smoothing window. The C# implementation handles arbitrary series lengths with a mixed-radix transform and direct transforms for prime factors.

A constant series has no dominant period and uses a one-year window. A peak period spanning nearly the complete record is explicitly flagged; with expanding early averages, the smoothed record may remain in its expanding stage throughout.

3. Two-stage smoothing

Mean[t] = (DOY[t-1] + DOY[t]) / 2
RollingMean[t] = average of the available Mean values
                 within the selected trailing window

The two-year step follows the supplied workbooks: current and preceding observation, aligned with the current observed year. The first observation has no two-year mean. Early rolling means expand until the selected window fills.

The paper describes a moving average selected from the FFT peak period. It does not explicitly prescribe the two-year pre-average. The software retains the workbooks' two-stage approach for continuity; the expanding early averages were selected for this implementation.

4. Two forecast estimates

Quadratic / historical “FFT” label

The programs fit a quadratic polynomial to the smoothed series and evaluate it at the next annual index, L+1. The inherited “FFT forecast” name refers to the FFT-selected smoothing that precedes the polynomial. The forecast is not a Fourier extrapolation.

Optimal Filtering-Based Model (OFBM)

The default uses the first recursive prediction after the observed record. The coefficient calculation retains the original translated filter's buffer initialization and unusual nested updates. Preserving those operations reproduces earlier calculations; it does not establish that the translated algorithm is a scientifically validated implementation of every published equation.

5. Dates and rounding

The raw fractional DOY is retained in CSV and JSON. Both the displayed integer DOY and the calendar date use the same half-up rounding rule. A result outside the forecast year's calendar range is flagged and receives no calendar date.

Historical comparison modes

--smoothing workbook uses the contiguous window extracted from workbook formulas, with consistent expanding early averages. It need not reproduce omitted early workbook rows. --smoothing legacy uses a retained rolling-mean snapshot and requires unchanged original inputs.

--timing legacy reproduces the earlier choice of polynomial evaluation at L and recursive output xp[L]. This differs from the default next-year indexing. Compatibility settings are intended for comparisons; the default workflow is the annual-update path.

What has been checked

The C# port passed 274 supported comparisons of Python smoothing/timing cases across all 55 regions, the original legacy recursive vectors, and input/calendar boundary tests. The largest measured forecast/fit difference was less than 0.000000000001 day. All compared calendar dates and rounded DOYs matched. The workbook importer reproduced all 2,741 Year/DOY observations.

The original Lake Melville VCPI workbook differs from its legacy text observations. The migrated CSV uses the workbook, as requested, and retains a warning. Other historical smoothing inconsistencies are documented in the source and metadata.

Interpret the result as guidance

These calculations use historical timing patterns. The website's 2026 examples are archived software outputs from observations ending in 2025. They are not a live ice outlook, a verified navigation product, or an official agency forecast. Review results against current conditions and inspect regional warnings before use.

Numerical agreement between implementations demonstrates reproducibility. A separate retrospective assessment would be needed to establish predictive accuracy for a particular event and period.
